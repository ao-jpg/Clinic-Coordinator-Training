Objective 1 · Patient flow

Patient Flow

Running the floor is one of the coordinator's two core duties, equal in weight to quality control. Order is not first come, first served. It is worst consequence, first seen.

01

The Coordinator's Two Jobs

When patients are in the building, the coordinator has two jobs, and only two jobs. Everyone else owns their own column. You own the whole floor.

1. Patient flow

You are the traffic control for the light-bar queue. Patients are brought back on time and in the correct order, seated in the appropriate chair, and you always know what is happening in every chair, not just the busy ones.

2. Clearing traffic jams

When the doctor is stuck in a room, you get him out: step in, take over the discussion, and direct him to the next chair. This applies to consultations too.

✓
While flow is running you are also spot-checking the work: no poking wires, soft tissue where it should be, and documentation complete and up to standard. (More on that in Preparation and Documentation.)
02

The Patient-Flow Cycle

Run this loop continuously. Do not wait for the next patient to appear before you start planning for them.

Look ahead›Choose location›Read the note›Prepare›Seat›Yellow: Chair Occupied›Start note›Ask + document›Comfort›Return to flow
Look at the schedule

Identify the next arrivals and the cluster coming after them.

Is each patient already here?

The plan changes if a patient is waiting versus still expected.

Choose the best chair or room

Use patient needs and future room demand, not simply the first empty chair.

Read the next-visit note

Find the planned procedure and any setup clues before you prepare.

Prepare the chair and supplies

Set up predictable items before seating. (See Preparation.)

Seat promptly, then mark the light bar yellow

Do not keep a patient waiting when an appropriate prepared chair is ready. Yellow means Chair Occupied.

Start a new visit note and ask about issues

Broken brackets, poking wires, pain, or concerns go into the note so the tech does not have to rediscover them.

Handle timing and comfort

Escalate any leave-by time early. Offer TV, make the patient comfortable.

Return to the open-bay computer

Resume looking ahead. After every patient is seated, ask: "Who is next?"

04

When to Delegate

Delegation is required whenever the person owning patient flow can no longer actively monitor the schedule and clinic. The most common reason is filling a clinical staffing need.

A tech is not coming in

and the coordinator must fill a chair.

A tech has fallen behind

and the coordinator needs to step into treatment to recover the schedule.

An unexpected clinical situation

requires the coordinator to assist directly.

Any task that pulls you away long enough

that schedule awareness or arrival prep would suffer.

Resist the urge to jump in

Stepping in to treat a patient yourself is a rare instance, not a tool for keeping the clinic on time. Being on time is all of our jobs, and it is not something the coordinator fixes by quietly taking a tech's patient.

What actually helps

Seat the patient and get the chair completely ready so the tech who is behind can jump right in and catch up. You keep flow moving without taking over the appointment.

What it must not become

Even seating for a tech cannot become the default. If a tech who is behind expects the coordinator to pick up their next patient, they stop hurrying, or they take their time. The responsibility to catch up stays with the tech.

!
What is not a handoff: walking away from the coordinator computer without naming a replacement, telling the room generally that you are helping elsewhere, assuming the closest free tech will watch the schedule, or asking someone to seat one patient without making clear who owns continuing flow. If you cannot keep watching flow, the role must be assigned before you leave.
☀
When coverage is predictable, plan it at the morning huddle: who is coordinator today, when they will need to cover a chair, who takes flow during that window, whether that person is truly free, and when control returns. Example: "Jocelyn is Clinic Coordinator. If she needs to cover a chair, the next available person takes patient flow until she is free again."

◆
The next person must actually be able to watch flow, use the computer, move through the clinic, and return to the coordinator station between seating tasks. If no listed person is truly available, escalate immediately. Never silently leave the position unowned.
06

The Handoff

Explicit, brief, and acknowledged. The outgoing coordinator does not leave until the receiving person knows the role is now theirs.

STEP 1
Identify

Select the highest available person in the coverage hierarchy.

STEP 2
State the transfer

Tell that person directly that you need them to take patient flow.

STEP 3
Give the picture

Only the high-priority facts: who is seated or waiting, near-term arrivals, any chair needing attention.

STEP 4
Get acknowledgment

They verbally confirm they have the role before you leave.

What good sounds like
Outgoing: "I need to cover Chair 3. Can you take Clinic Coordinator? Chair 4 is occupied, one patient is waiting, and our next two arrivals are close together." Receiving: "Yes, I have Clinic Coordinator."

A nod from across the room or a general announcement is weaker than a direct assignment and acknowledgment.

Handing control back

When the regular coordinator is free again, hand back just as clearly. The acting coordinator gives a brief status (who is seated, who is waiting, any active issue), the regular coordinator confirms they are taking the role back, and then resumes schedule monitoring. This prevents two people from both assuming the other owns flow.

The Light Bar

The light bar is the clinic's shared visual status. Keeping it current is part of seating a patient, not an administrative step to do later.

Yellow means Chair Occupied. When you seat a patient in Chair 4, the fourth slot turns yellow and reads "Chair Occupied." The rest of the bar does not change.

Priority order for doctor time

Order is not first come, first served. It is worst consequence, first seen.

1
Bondings and broken brackets

Retractors are in and the clock is running. These do not wait.

2
Consults

A new patient waiting is a start we can lose.

3
All other standard appointments

Adjustments, progress checks, routine visits.

4
Quick checks

Short by design. They absorb a wait better than anything else on the board.

!
The exception worth knowing: if an appointment needs a large block of doctor time, it can be saved for last on purpose. Clear the short work first so there is real, uninterrupted time for it. These usually qualify: gingivectomy, TAD placement, consult, direct bond, multiple broken brackets.
08

Chair and Room Rules

Chair assignment is not just what is empty. Protect room capacity for patients who need a room, and keep overflow in reserve.

Room candidates

Adults, children with long appointments, TCL patients, and children with special needs.

Chair 6

Do not default to Chair 6 for a waiting child just because it is open. If an appropriate room is free and not about to be needed, use the room.

Chair 7

Absolute overflow. Not the default place to put a patient because the clinic is busy. Use normal chairs and rooms strategically first.

✓
Ask yourself: does this patient benefit from a room? Is a room free now? Will a patient who needs a room more arrive soon? If I use this room now, do I create a predictable shortage in the next few arrivals?
09

On the Radio

A radio call has one purpose: give the coordinator an accurate ETA so the handoff to the doctor is already lined up.

Broken bracket, called in the way the coordinator can plan around
"Two broken, lower right five and six, ready in about four minutes."

Count and location tell you how much doctor time this will take and when to send him. "I have a broken bracket" does not. The priority is keeping retractors in for no more than a few minutes, so a good call includes: how many brackets are broken, which brackets, and how long the tech needs to prep before the doctor comes in.

Consults: treatment coordinators give you an ETA on every consult. Some need more time than others, so ask for a real number, not an optimistic one. You need to know who is queued for the doctor before he finishes the chair he is in.

10

Staying on Time

The best early-warning sign we have: if a tech is halfway through an appointment and still cannot say the objectives, they are already behind. That gets flagged on the radio then, not ten minutes later.

Front-load everything you can

  • Bring the patient back on time
  • Have the chair set up in advance
  • Read the chart in advance
  • Start the treatment card entry before seating

If you are already running late

Do not send the patient to go brush. It feels like a small delay and it is not. Adjust the plan to the time you actually have.

Late patient: look at the schedule first. The question is what can be done well in the time we have today without pushing every chair behind them off track.

◆
Getting us out on time is the coordinator's duty. Pacing the floor so the team breaks on time for lunch, and so the last patient of the day finishes without the schedule dragging, is part of the role. The coordinator is not off until the last patient has completed their appointment.
11

What the Coordinator Is Not For

Two habits quietly break flow. Both of them look like helping.

Not for servicing patients

The coordinator should not be seeing patients out of another tech's column. In the rare case you must seat a patient, hand off flow first: tell the other clinical lead, the lab coordinator, or the front desk that they are now running the light bar.

Not for writing your notes

You are responsible for the quality and completeness of notes. That is review, not authorship. You can help a tech work out the next objectives, but the tech who saw the patient writes the note. Every time.
